● This is a physics sanity-check question with a precise answer available in our
  sim — and yes, we can measure it directly in the v5.1 output. Let me first
  explain what he's asking, then actually run the measurement.

  The physics: in the solenoid (B = 1.4 T along z), a charged particle's
  transverse-plane trajectory is ideally a circle of radius R[cm] ≈ pT/(0.3·B) →
  ~119 cm for pT = 500 MeV. Across the TPC's radial span (20→78 cm) that arc
  has a sagitta of ~3.6 cm. "How close to a circle" = how big the residuals from
  a fitted circle are compared to that: deviations come from energy loss (slow
  inward spiral), multiple scattering in the gas, field-map non-uniformity, and
  occasionally a hard scatter/decay kink. Our truth chain supports this at two
  levels: pure Geant4 step coordinates (ntp_g4hit), and truth-tagged
  reconstructed clusters in the v5.1 production (island91 ntp_cluster +
  row-aligned ntp_truth with per-track gpt).

● Now it's drawn properly — sim_validation_plots/circle_geometry_sketch.png
  (producer island_post/circle_sketch.C). Here is the chain, one step at a time.
  The four numbers are not independent inputs: each one is computed from the 
  one before it, starting from only three facts — pT = 0.5 GeV (his example), B
  = 1.4 T (the solenoid), and the TPC's radial extent 20–78 cm (the field
  cages).

  Step 1: pT → R = 119 cm. A charged particle in a magnetic field feels a
  sideways force qvB, which acts as the centripetal force mv²/R of circular
  motion. Setting them equal: R = pT/(qB). In GeV-Tesla-meter units the
  constants q and c collapse into the number 0.3 (precisely 0.2998 — it is the
  speed of light in the unit conversion, not a tunable): R[m] = pT/(0.3·B) = 
  0.5/(0.3×1.4) = 1.19 m = 119 cm. My earlier message wrote this as "R[cm]" —
  with that typo 0.5/0.42 = 1.19 indeed doesn't give 119, which is why it looked
  like a random number. It's meters. Sanity anchor: this is the same formula 
  already in our pipeline — PT_LOOP = 0.164 GeV in islandize91.C is exactly 0.3
  × 1.4 × 0.39 m, i.e. the pT whose circle radius is 0.39 m = half of 78 cm, the
  "curls before the field cage" condition.

  One thing the sketch makes obvious, which I suspect was the real source of
  "unrelated": there are two different radii in this problem. Little r (20, 78
  cm) is distance from the beamline — where the detector sits. Big R (119 cm) is
  the radius of the track's own circle, whose center is nowhere near the
  beamline (it's at (0, 119) in the sketch). A 119 cm circle fits through a 78
  cm detector with no contradiction because they're circles around different
  centers.

  Step 2: which piece of that circle do we see? The track starts at the vertex
  and the TPC only records it while 20 < r < 78 cm — the thick blue arc in the
  sketch. Because 2R = 238 cm > 78 cm, the circle punches through the whole TPC
  and exits (that's the definition of a "track"; a looper is the opposite case).

  Step 3: the chord, c = 58.8 cm. Straight line between the arc's entry and exit
  points (red dashed). Since the track is only gently curved, the chord is
  approximately just the radial gap it crossed: 78 − 20 = 58 cm; doing the
  circle geometry exactly gives 58.8 cm.

  Step 4: chord + R → sagitta = 3.6 cm. The sagitta s is how far the arc bulges
  away from its own chord (the little double arrow). It's pure Pythagoras: the
  triangle made of the circle center, the chord midpoint, and a chord endpoint
  has sides (R − s), c/2, and hypotenuse R, so R² = (R−s)² + (c/2)², which gives
  s = R − √(R² − c²/4) ≈ c²/(8R) = 58.8²/(8×119) = 3.6 cm. So 3.6 cm is
  literally computed from the 119 and the 58.8 — that's the sense in which all
  the numbers are one chain: pT → R → (with the TPC radii) chord → sagitta.

  Why I quote the sagitta at all: it answers "compared to what?" for your
  supervisor's question. The sagitta is the total amount of "circle-ness" the
  TPC can see — a straight line would miss the points by up to 3.6 cm. The
  measured scatter of the truth points around the fitted circle is 0.002 cm. So
  "how close is the trajectory to a circle" gets a dimensionless answer:
  deviations are ~5×10⁻⁴ of the curvature signal itself. (It's also, not
  coincidentally, the quantity every momentum measurement actually measures — pT
  resolution is sagitta resolution.)

❯ what is SAGITTA                                                               

● Sagitta is Latin for "arrow", and the name is the picture: take an archery
  bow. The bent bow is the arc, the bowstring is the straight chord between its
  two ends, and the arrow lying against the string points from the middle of the
  string to the middle of the bow. The length of that gap — how far the arc 
  bulges away from its own chord at the midpoint — is the sagitta. That's the
  little black double-arrow in the sketch I just made.

  Formally, for a circular arc of radius R whose endpoints are connected by a
  chord of length c:

  - exact: s = R − √(R² − (c/2)²)  (Pythagoras on the triangle center–chord
  midpoint–chord endpoint)
  - small-angle shortcut: s ≈ c²/(8R) — bigger chord → more bulge, bigger radius
  (straighter track) → less bulge.

  In our case: the track's visible piece inside the TPC is the bow (R = 119 cm),
  the 58.8 cm chord is the bowstring, and the bulge between them is s ≈ 3.6 cm.

  It's a standard word in tracking because it's the physically measured thing: a
  detector never sees the whole circle, only a short arc, and the only evidence
  that the arc is curved — hence the only handle on momentum — is that it sags
  3.6 cm away from a straight line. That's why I used it as the yardstick for
  your supervisor's question: "how close to a circle" = residuals (20 µm)
  compared against the total curvature signal available (3.6 cm).
